The GitHub
Gospel

The whole story of the Bible — Eden, the fall, the cross, the resurrection, and what it means to be saved — told in the language of repositories, commits, branches, and pull requests. Not because the Bible is about software, but because both are about the same thing: a history, a relationship, and whether you're still connected to the source.

The analogy key

Every person, in this telling, is a repository — a life with a history. God is the Maintainer and the remote: the source everything came from, and the only place a life is kept safe beyond its own machine. Keep this key in mind and the rest of the story reads itself.

Chapter 01 · Genesis 1–2

Eden: the initial commit was “very good”

In the beginning, there was no divergence. Adam and Eve were created in the image of God — made from His own design, living in His garden, in unbroken connection with the Maintainer. Their history had no corruption in it. Every commit was clean. When God looked at everything He had made, the review was simple.

“And God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good.”

GENESIS 1:31 · ESV

There was only one protected rule — one file that was off-limits. The tree of the knowledge of good and evil wasn't a trap; it was the one place where trust was actually tested. Obedience you can't refuse isn't trust at all. Everything else in the garden was a gift. That one tree was the relationship, in a single command: will you stay connected to the source, on the source's terms?

In repo terms

Humanity begins as a perfect clone of the Maintainer's intent — 0 conflicts, full access, nothing hidden, nothing broken. The story doesn't start with a bug. It starts with a choice.

Chapter 02 · Genesis 3

The Fall: corruption enters the log

The serpent's pitch was, in essence, a promise of root access: you will be like God. You won't need upstream. You can be your own maintainer. Adam and Eve took the commit — and the corruption wasn't just in that one act. It entered the history itself, and everything built on top of it afterward.

“Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned…”

ROMANS 5:12 · ESV

This is the part the analogy explains better than almost anything else: you cannot fix a corrupted history by making good commits from now on. The bad commits are already in the log, and you cannot rewrite a log you don't own. Every descendant of Adam inherits the diverged fork — born local-only, born cut off, born with corrupted commits already in the history before they've typed a line themselves.

“For all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God.”

ROMANS 3:23 · ESV

The immediate result in Eden was separation. Adam and Eve hid. Then they were sent out of the garden — not because God stopped loving the repo, but because a corrupted branch living forever in that state would be a curse, not a blessing. The exile was, in a strange way, mercy: the machine would one day die, but the story wasn't over.

Chapter 03 · Genesis 4 — Malachi

The long wait: God keeps the PR promised

Most of the Old Testament is the long middle of this story. God doesn't abandon the fork. He chooses a people — Israel — gives them the Law, and keeps repeating a promise: a fix is coming, and it will come from inside the corrupted line itself. A descendant of Eve. A shoot from the stump of Jesse. A Branch.

The Law, in this analogy, works like a linter that never stops reporting errors. It is good — it shows exactly where the history is broken — but a linter has never once fixed the code it diagnoses. Sacrifices were a temporary patch, applied over and over, pointing forward to a permanent one.

“For by works of the law no human being will be justified in his sight, since through the law comes knowledge of sin.”

ROMANS 3:20 · ESV

And through the prophets, God says the quiet part out loud about what the final fix will have to involve — not editing around the corrupted commits, but Someone taking them onto Himself.

“But he was pierced for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his wounds we are healed.”

ISAIAH 53:5 · ESV

Chapter 04 · The Gospels

The Fix: authored by the Maintainer's Son

Here is where the analogy turns into good news. The fix could not come from any of us — every one of us is working from inside the corrupted history. So God did the one thing no maintainer normally does: He entered the repository Himself. Jesus is God the Son, born into Adam's line, living inside the diverged fork — and committing a perfect history. No corruption. Not one bad commit, in thought, word, or deed, across an entire human life.

Then, at the cross, the exchange happened. The one clean history took on all the corrupted ones. Jesus didn't just die near our sin; the Bible says He was made sin for us, and that God laid on Him the iniquity of us all. His final words were, fittingly, a commit message:

“Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!”

LUKE 23:46 · ESV

And then — the part that separates Christianity from every mere philosophy — the resurrection. Death tried to delete the one clean repo, and it couldn't hold it. The tomb came back empty because a history with no corruption in it has nothing for death to claim. The resurrection is God's own review stamp on the fix: accepted, verified, it works.

“I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live.”

JOHN 11:25 · ESV

“For Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring us to God.”

1 PETER 3:18 · ESV

Why it had to be Him

A fix authored by a corrupted branch is just more corrupted commits. The fix had to be authored by someone with a clean history and with the authority to apply it to everyone else's. Only God, in the flesh, is both.

Chapter 05 · The offer

Being saved: accepting a PR you didn't write

So the fix exists. It is finished, reviewed, and raised from the dead. What does a person actually do? In the analogy: God sends you a pull request — and Christ is the fix in it. His life, death, and resurrection resolve every conflict in your branch, including the ones you could never resolve yourself.

You don't author the fix. Faith is accepting the PR. That is precisely what “not by works” means:

“For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast.”

EPHESIANS 2:8–9 · ESV

Try it, in miniature. This is the whole transaction:

fix: resolve every corrupted commit in your history #1
jesus wants to merge grace into you:main · authored 2,000 years ago · still open
● OPEN — awaiting your review
your-history.log− every sin, unfixable by you
your-history.log+ blotted out — “I will not remember your sins”
book-of-life.txt+ your name, added
connection+ live sync opened — the Spirit dwells in you

Merging does three things at once: your bad commits are blotted out (that's justification — the repo declared clean), your name is added to the collaborators list (that's the Book of Life), and your repo is pushed to the remote — backed up where the laptop dying can't touch it.

✓ Merged. And notice — it's more than a backup:
  • Adopted, not just rescued — your repo moves into His organization: “he gave the right to become children of God” (John 1:12).
  • A live sync, not a snapshot — the Holy Spirit indwells you; the connection stays open, and the slow work of cleaning up daily commits (sanctification) begins.
  • A new creation — “If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation” (2 Cor 5:17). Not a rescued file. A remade one.

One honest note on the illustration above: clicking a button on a web page saves no one. The real merge happens the unglamorous way the Bible describes — a person hears what Christ has done, believes it, and entrusts themselves to Him. The button is just the analogy, doing its job: showing that your part is receiving, not achieving.

Chapter 06 · The other outcome

Those who don't accept: the PR stays open

God doesn't force the merge. The fork owner has to accept it — that's what makes it a relationship instead of an overwrite. If a person never does, the branch stays diverged, the corrupted commits are never blotted out, and the name is never added to the collaborators list.

And when the laptop dies, there is no remote backup and no restore. That is the Bible's own Book of Life language:

“And if anyone's name was not found written in the book of life, he was thrown into the lake of fire.”

REVELATION 20:15 · ESV

At the final audit — “the books were opened” (Rev 20:12) — the code gets reviewed as-is, because the fix was never applied. The traditional view, held by most churches through history, is that hell is eternal separation:

“They will suffer the punishment of eternal destruction, away from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of his might.”

2 THESSALONIANS 1:9 · ESV

Notice what that means in the analogy: it's not God reaching in to corrupt someone's repo. It's the fork staying cut off from upstream, forever — the separation is the punishment. Christians do argue about the details — some hold that the wicked are finally destroyed rather than tormented forever — but the part they agree on is the separation itself, and that it follows from refusing the merge while it was offered.

Chapter 07 · The hardest question

Predestination & free will: two chairs, one repo

Every Christian eventually hits the tension. The Bible says God chose His people before the foundation of the world (Eph 1:4–5) — and it also says “whoever believes” may come (John 3:16), as if the choice were really ours. Calvinists and Arminians have argued the point for five centuries. The repo analogy doesn't settle it — but it does show how both can be true at once, because they describe the same events from two different seats.

The Maintainer's chair

God's-eye view

The Maintainer wrote the codebase, owns the remote, and stands outside the commit timeline entirely. From His seat, nothing is uncertain and nothing is discovered as it happens — He authored the story, chose the collaborators before the first commit, and guarantees the final merge of everything He started. “Those whom he predestined he also called… and those whom he justified he also glorified” (Rom 8:30) — past tense, from a seat where it's already done.

The contributor's keyboard

Our-eye view

From inside the repo, you experience real keystrokes. The PR genuinely arrives, the decision genuinely sits in front of you, and nobody accepts it on your behalf. When a person believes, they really believe — freely, willingly, as their own act. And here's the twist the Bible insists on: even the willingness was given. “No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him” (John 6:44). The hand that clicks merge is yours. The drawing that brought you to the button was His.

An author and a character don't compete for credit for the same sentence. Shakespeare isn't diminished because Hamlet really deliberates; Hamlet's deliberation isn't fake because Shakespeare wrote it. God's sovereignty and human responsibility work the same way — not 50/50, but 100/100, at different levels of the same story.

“Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you, both to will and to work for his good pleasure.”

PHILIPPIANS 2:12–13 · ESV

Both halves of that verse, in one breath. Work it out — for God is the one working in you. The analogy's honest conclusion is the same one most thoughtful Christians land on: hold both, refuse to delete either, and let the tension be a sign you're describing something bigger than a flowchart.

Chapter 08 · A happy discovery

The words were already in the Bible

The strangest thing about this analogy is how often the Bible got there first. Commit, branch, and pull aren't modern words we pasted onto an ancient book — they're the book's own vocabulary.

commit

“Commit your way to the LORD; trust in him, and he will act” (Ps 37:5). Committing is entrusting — handing your history over to someone else's keeping. It's what Jesus did on the cross (Luke 23:46), and it's what a believer does with their whole life.

branch

A Messianic title, centuries before Git: “There shall come forth a shoot from the stump of Jesse, and a branch from his roots shall bear fruit” (Isa 11:1). And Jesus: “I am the vine; you are the branches” (John 15:5). Grafted into the living Branch — or a dead fork.

pull

The Bible's word is draw: “No one can come to me unless the Father who sent me draws him” (John 6:44). God pulls. The pull request is His drawing; our acceptance is the merge. Both directions of the same word carry the whole doctrine of salvation.

The language fits because both vocabularies are about the same realities: a history you can't rewrite, a connection you can't live without, and a source you either stay joined to or drift from.

Chapter 09 · Honesty about the tool

Where the analogy breaks down

Every analogy limps somewhere, and a good one says where. This one is a teaching tool, not a doctrine — the Bible is the authority, and where the picture and the Book disagree, the Book wins.

Three honest limits

  • A soul isn't a file. It's immaterial. “Stored with God” means sustained by Him, moment by moment — not parked on a disk somewhere. God is not a server, and heaven is not a data center.
  • God is not distant. A remote repository is far away by definition. The real God is nearer than the analogy allows — He indwells believers, hears prayer, and is personally present in a way no sync connection imitates.
  • The resurrection is more than a restore. A restore brings back the same files. The resurrection brings a new, glorified body — “sown in dishonor, raised in glory” (1 Cor 15:43). The end of the story isn't recovering the old repo. It's a new creation, where “the dwelling place of God is with man” (Rev 21:3) and no one needs a backup ever again.

Which is, in the end, the best news in the whole story. Repositories crash, laptops die, companies fold, and formats rot. The promise underneath this analogy is that the people whose names are in that Book are kept by Someone who does none of those things.

“He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away.”

REVELATION 21:4 · ESV
